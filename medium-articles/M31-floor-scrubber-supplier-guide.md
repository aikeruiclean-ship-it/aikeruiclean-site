How to Choose a Floor Scrubber Supplier: Factory-Direct vs Dealer Network

Most buyers spend weeks comparing machine specifications and almost no time comparing the companies selling them. That is backwards. The same ride-on scrubber specification can cost you 9,000 dollars or 35,000 dollars depending purely on which layer of the supply chain you buy from. The machine is the easy part. The supplier decision is where the money is.

This guide walks through the five supplier types you will actually encounter, the seven factors worth evaluating, and what the price gap really represents.

The five supplier types

Factory-direct manufacturer

A company that owns the production line and sells its own machines without an intermediary. You are buying from the factory that welds the chassis, fits the pumps and tests the unit. Because there is no importer or dealer margin layered on top, pricing typically lands 30 to 50 percent below equivalent Western brand machines.

Trade-off: service reach is your responsibility. There is no local technician network, so you need to plan for spare parts logistics.

Authorized dealer

A franchised reseller of a brand such as Tennant, Kärcher or Nilfisk. You get factory-backed warranty, trained technicians and fast local parts. You also pay for two layers of margin: the brand's, and the dealer's. For facilities that cannot tolerate downtime, that premium can be worth it.

Trading company

A reseller that does not manufacture anything. It buys from a factory and marks up. Some are competent and add real value in logistics and documentation. Others simply insert themselves between you and a factory you could have contacted directly. The distinguishing question is simple: can they show you the production line, or only a catalogue.

E-commerce marketplace

Platforms that list industrial equipment from many sellers. Convenient for small orders and fast payment, but specification control is weak and after-sales support is unpredictable. Rarely the right channel for fleet purchases.

Used and refurbished dealer

A legitimate option for buyers with tight capital budgets and in-house maintenance capability. Inspect the battery pack, brush deck and squeegee assembly specifically, because those are the parts that determine remaining service life.

Seven factors worth evaluating

| Factor | Why it matters | What to ask for |
| Supply model | Reveals how many margin layers you are paying | Is the supplier the factory owner or a reseller? |
| Certifications | Determines whether the machine clears your compliance requirements | CE certificate and ISO 9001 document, dated |
| Lead time | Affects when your facility actually gets clean floors | Production time plus shipping time, in writing |
| Spare parts access | The hidden cost that dwarfs purchase price over five years | Will consumables and brushes be available in three years, at what price? |
| MOQ and terms | Sets the entry point for wholesale pricing | One unit sample, five units wholesale, 30 or 70 payment |
| Export experience | Predicts how much documentation trouble you inherit | Bill of lading from a recent shipment, and which countries |
| Customisation | Whether the machine fits your floor or you adapt to it | Can they change deck width, battery type and branding? |

What the price gap actually represents

Take a ride-on scrubber with a 40 inch working width and 120 litre tanks. Through a brand dealer network it costs roughly 18,000 to 35,000 dollars. From a factory-direct supplier, the same specification costs roughly 9,000 to 18,000 dollars.

That gap is not a quality statement. The components inside are frequently the same imported pumps, motors and batteries. The gap is the cost of the distribution layer: import duties, warehousing, dealer overhead and two sets of margin.

For a single machine, the difference may not justify giving up local service. For a fleet of three or more, it typically saves 25,000 to 50,000 dollars, which is enough to fund a comprehensive spare parts stock and still come out ahead.

Three negotiation points that actually move the number

Ask for the specification, not the headline price. Suppliers quote differently: one quotes a bare machine, another includes batteries and charger, a third adds a squeegee and brush set. Normalise every quote to the same configuration before comparing.

Trade volume for terms. Factories will move on payment terms before they move on unit price. Accepting 30 percent deposit and 70 percent before shipment instead of 100 percent advance is usually worth more than a few percent off the unit cost.

Order one sample unit. A supplier confident in its production will support a single-unit sample order. A reseller will push you straight to a container. Use the sample order to test both the machine and how the supplier handles a small, inconvenient order.

The practical conclusion

There is no universally correct supplier type. Facilities with high downtime cost and low volume should stay with authorized dealers and accept the premium. Distributors, rental fleets and facilities buying three or more units should buy factory-direct and invest the saved margin into their own spare parts inventory.

The mistake to avoid is buying at factory-direct prices from a company that is not actually a factory. Verify the business licence, request a live video walkthrough of the production line, and ask for a recent bill of lading. A real manufacturer will agree to all three without hesitation.
